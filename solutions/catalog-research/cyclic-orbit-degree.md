# Exact invariant degrees on cyclic Fourier supports

**Result.** For an unprojected real cyclic signal, an integer lattice computes the exact remaining phase fibre after observing all invariants through degree d ≥ 2. Its rank decides finite-list recovery; its index decides unique recovery. For every even N ≥ 4, a signal whose nonzero Fourier frequencies are **all the odd frequencies**, with an optional DC component, needs degree **four** for both finite-list and unique recovery. Degree three leaves a continuous ambiguity. This explains a known pair of exceptional hexachords and gives their missing fourth-order measurement.

This is a **restricted answer**, with established invariant-theory antecedents, to Wein's Question 4.2.8. It does not settle arbitrary group representations, projections or mixtures. No novelty is claimed for the lattice method or the full odd-support specialization.

## Exact thesis correspondence

Alexander Spence Wein, *Statistical estimation in the presence of group actions* (2018), Question 4.2.8, printed pp. 123–124, asks for the degree needed to uniquely resolve a fixed signal's orbit or reduce it to finitely many orbits. Exact extraction ID: `72184819523abfd5529a`; local text; [MIT thesis](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/117314). Here the group is Z_N acting by cyclic coordinate shifts on V = R^N, without projection or heterogeneity. The degree-d moment tensor is the group average of x raised to its d-fold tensor power. Its entries span the homogeneous invariants of degree d, by Reynolds averaging. Thus the invariants considered below are precisely the thesis's moment information in this model.

A pair of distinct translation classes with the same translation 3-deck is

\[
A=\{0,1,2,4,5,9\},\qquad B=\{0,1,2,5,9,10\}\subset\mathbb Z_{12}.
\]

Use translation as the group equivalence. Inversion is a different operation: replacing translation by translation-and-inversion would merge this particular pair and change the question. Also distinguish the analytical 3-deck, including repeated offsets, from the multiset of distinct three-element substructures. The controls below use the analytical deck.

## A support-specific phase criterion

Write F_k = Σ_j x_j exp(−2πijk/N). Degree one recovers F_0. Degree two recovers every |F_k|², hence the exact nonzero support and all amplitudes. Let S exclude frequency zero and contain exactly the remaining nonzero frequencies. Reality gives S = −S and F_{−k} = conjugate(F_k).

Compare x with a signal y having the same degree-one and degree-two data. For k in S put z_k = F_k(y)/F_k(x), so |z_k| = 1 and z_k z_{−k} = 1. For a self-inverse frequency N/2 this is z_k² = 1; its sign must be retained.

In the free integer group Z^S, let L_d be generated by:

1. Every e_k + e_{−k}, including 2e_{N/2} when that coordinate is present.
2. Every nonnegative exponent vector a with Σ_k a_k ≤ d and Σ_k k a_k = 0 modulo N.

**Phase-fibre theorem.** The complete fibre of the degree-at-most-d invariant map over x is in bijection with

\[
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb Z^S/L_d,\mathbb S^1).
\]

Let r = |S|, let ρ be the rank of L_d, and let h = N/gcd(N,S) be the size of a translation orbit on this support. Then:

- If ρ < r, the fibre contains a torus of dimension r−ρ and infinitely many translation orbits: finite-list recovery fails.
- If ρ = r, write I = [Z^S:L_d]. There are exactly I/h translation orbits in the fibre. In particular, unique recovery holds exactly when I = h.

For S empty use h = 1 and I = 1; the constant signal already resolves at degree one. The criterion otherwise starts at degree two and makes no claim that the amplitudes are available at degree one.

**Proof.** Fourier coordinates are an invertible complex linear change of coordinates. Averaging a Fourier monomial over cyclic shifts retains it exactly when its total frequency is zero modulo N, and kills it otherwise. These retained monomials span the complexification of the invariant space; knowing their real and imaginary parts is equivalent to knowing the real invariants. Monomials using a zero Fourier coefficient vanish for both signals. DC factors are already fixed and add no phase equations. All others agree exactly when ∏_k z_k^{a_k} = 1 for the generators in item 2. Item 1 imposes precisely the reality constraints. Therefore phase assignments are the stated character group. Conversely, every such assignment gives a real signal by inverse Fourier transform with the same amplitudes and invariant data.

Smith normal form writes Z^S/L_d as a free group of rank r−ρ plus finite cyclic factors. Its character group has the asserted torus dimension and, in the full-rank case, I elements. Translations multiply phases by N-th roots with weights k and form a subgroup of size h. This subgroup acts freely on every phase assignment, because all coordinates indexed by S are nonzero. Quotienting therefore divides the finite count by h. ∎

The [solver](cyclic_phase_lattice.py) implements these integer calculations with SymPy. It preserves torsion and distinguishes rank from index; a real-valued Jacobian rank alone would not compute the finite orbit count. Enumerating low-degree monomials can become expensive as support and degree grow; this implementation is not a polynomial-time algorithm in arbitrary input degree.

## A complete answer for full odd support

**Theorem.** Let N ≥ 4 be even and suppose S = {1,3,…,N−1}. For every real x with exactly this nonzero Fourier support, the smallest degree for finite-list recovery and for unique recovery is four. DC may be present or absent.

**Lower bound.** An odd number of odd frequencies cannot sum to zero modulo the even number N. A degree-three invariant therefore either contains DC, in which case it only repeats fixed lower-degree information, or cannot occur. At degree two the only nonzero-frequency invariant monomials are conjugate pairs. Thus data through degree three fix amplitudes but impose no additional phase constraints. There are floor(N/4) independent circle phases, and possibly a Nyquist sign when N = 2 modulo 4. Since floor(N/4) ≥ 1 and translation is finite, the fibre contains infinitely many orbits.

**Upper bound.** Quartic monomials F_a F_b F_c F_{−a−b−c} are invariant and nonzero whenever a,b,c are odd. Equality of their values implies

\[
z_{a+b+c}=z_a z_b z_c.
\]

With a = b = 1 this gives z_{k+2} = z_1² z_k for every odd k, interpreted modulo N. Starting at k = 1, induction gives z_k = z_1^k for odd representatives 1 ≤ k < N. Reality at k = N−1 then gives z_1^{N−1} = z_1^{−1}, hence z_1^N = 1. Consequently these phases are exactly a cyclic translation. No other orbit remains. ∎

For N = 12 just three quartic phase equations already suffice: the zero-sum frequency multisets (1,1,1,9), (1,1,3,7), and (1,1,5,5). They give z_3 = z_1³, z_5 = z_1⁵ and z_1¹² = 1, respectively. Lower-degree invariants already fix DC, amplitudes and conjugate phases.

This failure can occur for **strictly positive** real signals, too. Choose positive DC with F_0 > Σ_{k≠0}|F_k| and nonzero amplitudes at all odd frequencies. Every reality-compatible phase assignment then has x_j ≥ (F_0−Σ|F_k|)/N > 0. Degree three still leaves a continuous positive family. Positivity alone does not fill the Fourier hole. This is not a counterexample to Baechler's optical conjecture: that problem has a different calibrated forward operator and admissible domain.

Full odd support is essential. For example, when N ≥ 5 and S = {1,N−1}, every invariant below degree N is phase-blind: a zero-sum monomial of degree less than N must have balanced counts of the two frequencies. Degree N first supplies F_1^N and resolves the translation orbit. Thus real cyclic signals in general need not resolve at degree four.

## The missing measurement in this example

Exact cyclotomic arithmetic confirms that both indicator signals 1_A and 1_B have Fourier support {0,1,3,5,7,9,11}. The theorem therefore gives their exact ambient R¹² recovery degree: four for both list and unique recovery. The degree-three ambiguity is larger than just the two binary sets; the ambient fibre has three continuous phase dimensions.

A time-domain invariant that separates the binary pair is

\[
Q(x)=\sum_{t\in\mathbb Z_{12}}x_t x_{t+1}x_{t+2}x_{t+4}.
\]

It gives Q(1_A) = 1 and Q(1_B) = 0. A has the translated pattern {0,1,2,4} once, at t = 0; B has none. This is a concrete fourth-order sensor for this ambiguity, without estimating an entire bispectrum. The Fourier quartics above, rather than this one scalar alone, establish completeness on the whole real support class.

## Attribution, controls and remaining work

The nearest general antecedent is [Domokos, *Degree bound for separating invariants of abelian groups* (2016), Theorem 2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06597): separating invariant monomials are characterized by the integer groups generated by zero-sum sequences on supports. The fibre calculation here is a real cyclic specialization with conjugacy and finite-list cardinalities made explicit. [Jaming–Kolountzakis, *Reconstruction of functions from their triple correlations* (2003), §3 and the p = 2 remark](https://nyjm.albany.edu/j/2003/9-12.pdf), already uses Fourier-support phase propagation and a fourth-order recurrence. Its discussion also credits the established four-deck reconstruction theorem for cyclic subsets. These precedents rule out treating the local binary enumeration or recurrence as a new general theorem.

[Edidin–Katz (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00009) proves degree-three separation for generic orbits in finite-group regular representations. Our support class lies in a proper subspace and does not contradict that theorem. Wein's own thesis also gives general algebraic characterizations and algorithms; the contribution here is an executable support-specific answer and a precise explanation of this ambiguity.

This also checks degree-four separation of every binary class in Z₁₂. Hand proofs, rather than finite meshes, establish the continuous assertions.
