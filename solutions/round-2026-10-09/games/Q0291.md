# Q291: every equilibrium has at least two scissors players

## Result

**Full affirmative proof.** For every integer `m >= 2`, each mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of the specified imbalanced `m`-player rock-paper-scissors game has at least two players who assign positive probability to scissors. No symmetry of the players' mixed strategies is assumed, and pure strategies and zero probabilities are included.

The proof below resolves the statement of Q291, including the arbitrary-player bound in Maimon's equivalent infeasibility conjecture. It does not prove the separate conjectures about recursively blown-up games with more than three actions.

Evidence: a self-contained payoff comparison, independent mathematical review, and exact finite controls. Date: 2026-10-09. No publication or external peer review is claimed.

## Primary statement

The original source is Itai Maimon, [*Homological Codes, their Extensions, and Certain Unfair Games*](https://escholarship.org/content/qt84c9t3c3/qt84c9t3c3.pdf), UC San Diego PhD thesis (2024), §5.7, especially Definition 5.7.3 and Conjecture 5.7.6. The later paper [*Different Forms of Imbalance in Strongly Playable Discrete Games II: Multi-Player RPS Games*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.13736), version 1 (2025), gives the same game in Definition 2.1 and the relevant conjecture in §3, Conjecture 3.3. The latter source proves a bounded-player statement computationally and leaves the all-player statement conjectural.

The rules used here are exactly these. If both rock and scissors occur, rock wins. With only rock and paper present, paper wins. With only paper and scissors present, scissors wins. If all players choose the same action, all win. When exactly `k` players choose the winning action, each receives `m/k - 1`, and every loser receives `-1`.

Adding 1 and dividing by the positive constant `m` preserves every best-response comparison. Work throughout with the **winning share** payoff: each of the `k` winners receives `1/k`, and each loser receives zero.

## Proof

### No scissors player is impossible

Suppose all opponents of some player use only rock and paper. Against every pure profile of those opponents, paper strictly beats rock in expected-share comparison:

- If all opponents choose rock, choosing paper earns 1, while choosing rock earns `1/m`.
- If some opponent chooses paper, choosing rock earns zero, while choosing paper earns a positive share.

Thus paper strictly dominates rock against any such mixed opponent profile. In an equilibrium with no scissors player, everyone must therefore choose paper with probability one. A deviation to scissors then earns 1 instead of `1/m`. Since `m >= 2`, this contradicts equilibrium.

### A unique scissors player is also impossible

Suppose player `h` is the only player assigning positive probability to scissors, and write that probability as `s > 0`. All of `h`'s opponents use only rock and paper. The strict comparison above implies that `h` assigns zero probability to rock. Thus `h` chooses paper with probability `1-s` and scissors with probability `s`; the case `s=1` remains allowed.

For each ordinary player `i != h`, put `q_i = Pr(i chooses paper)`. Their random choices are independent, as required for a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium. Write

$$
Q=\prod_{i\ne h}q_i,
\qquad
A=\mathbb E\left[\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i\ne h}I_i}\right],
$$

where the independent Bernoulli variable `I_i` has parameter `q_i`.

If `h` chooses paper, paper always wins and its number of players is `1+sum I_i`; hence `A` is `h`'s payoff for paper. If `h` chooses scissors, it wins alone precisely when all the other players choose paper; hence `Q` is its payoff for scissors. Since scissors belongs to `h`'s equilibrium support,

$$
Q\ge A>0.\tag{1}
$$

In particular, every `q_i` is strictly positive. Notice that only the weak best-response inequality is needed; equality of the two payoffs is not assumed.

Fix any ordinary player `i`. Let

$$
T_i=\sum_{j\ne h,i}I_j,
\quad
B_i=\mathbb E\left[\frac1{2+T_i}\right],
\quad
D_i=\mathbb E\left[\frac1{1+T_i}\right],
\quad
Q_{-i}=\prod_{j\ne h,i}q_j.
$$

Empty products equal one, so these definitions include `m=2`. Conditioning on `I_i` gives

$$
A=(1-q_i)D_i+q_i B_i\ge B_i,\tag{2}
$$

because `D_i >= B_i`.

Now calculate player `i`'s payoff for its two possible choices of interest:

$$
U_i(P)=(1-s)B_i.\tag{3}
$$

Indeed, if `h` chooses scissors, paper cannot win, while if `h` chooses paper there are `2+T_i` paper players.

If `i` instead chooses scissors, it loses whenever any other ordinary player chooses rock. If all those ordinary players choose paper, it wins alone when `h` chooses paper and shares with `h` when `h` chooses scissors. Therefore

$$
U_i(S)=\left(1-s+\frac{s}{2}\right)Q_{-i}
=\left(1-\frac{s}{2}\right)Q_{-i}.\tag{4}
$$

Combining (1)-(3) and using `Q=q_i Q_-i <= Q_-i`,

$$
U_i(P)
\le (1-s)A
\le (1-s)Q
\le (1-s)Q_{-i}
<\left(1-\frac{s}{2}\right)Q_{-i}
=U_i(S).\tag{5}
$$

The strict inequality holds because `s > 0` and `Q_-i > 0`. But `q_i > 0` means that paper is in player `i`'s support, so it must be a best response. Equation (5) contradicts that requirement. This proves that no equilibrium has exactly one scissors player. Together with the first case, it proves the theorem. QED.

## Quantitative form of the contradiction

Equations (3)-(5) show that, whenever the unique scissors player's scissors action is a best response,

$$
U_i(S)-U_i(P)\ge\frac{s}{2}Q_{-i}>0
$$

for every other player. In the original payoff normalization the gain is at least `m s Q_-i / 2`. Since `A >= 1/m` and `Q_-i >= Q >= A`, the gain is at least `s/2` in the original units.

The comparison avoids reducing the other players to identical mixtures, solving polynomial systems, or imposing an upper bound on the number of players.
