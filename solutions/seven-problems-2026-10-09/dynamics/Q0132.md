# Q132: convergence with unbounded waiting times

**Status: partial.** Two sufficient conditions are proved below. The first allows arbitrary positive Leontief weights and many schedules with unbounded waiting times. The second allows every fair schedule when the weights factor through a common weight for each project; in particular, it applies to arbitrary weights when the donor–project incidence graph is a forest. Neither theorem settles all weighted profiles under all fair schedules.

The proofs and the separate review are internal mathematical work with AI assistance, not external peer review. No historical-priority claim is made.

## 1. Source and precise target

[Greger's thesis](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1759811/document.pdf), Open Problem 5, printed page 99, asks whether Theorem 5.24 remains true when every donor appears infinitely often, with no uniform bound on the waiting time. Theorem 5.24 is on printed pages 66–67; its proof and the already established binary-weight case follow in Section 5.2.3. The thesis PDF was retrieved and these passages inspected on 9 October 2026.

The corresponding paper, [Brandt–Greger–Segal-Halevi–Suksompong, *Coordinating Charitable Donations with Leontief Preferences*, arXiv:2305.10286v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.10286v3), gives the dynamics in Section 6.1, the bounded-waiting theorem as Theorem 3, and its proof in Appendix D.1. Existence, uniqueness of the aggregate equilibrium, and its identification with the Nash-welfare allocation are background results from the source. The binary-weight fair-schedule conclusion is also background, not a contribution of this report.

There are finitely many donors and projects. Donor $i$ has budget $C_i>0$, support $A_i\ne\varnothing$, and weights $v_{ix}>0$ on $A_i$. Write

$$
 u_i(\delta)=\min_{x\in A_i}\frac{\delta_x}{v_{ix}},\qquad
 B=\sum_i C_i,\qquad m=\left|\bigcup_i A_i\right|.
$$

Projects valued by nobody can be omitted. Multiplying all weights of one donor by a positive constant does not change her best response. A state $X=(X_i)_i$ records each donor's allocation, with $X_i\ge0$, support in $A_i$, and total $C_i$. Its aggregate is $\delta=\sum_iX_i$. Initially all rows are zero; discard the finite prefix ending when every donor has first appeared. All subsequent states belong to the compact product of these supported simplices. A schedule is **fair** if every donor appears infinitely often.

## 2. Exact best responses and continuity

Given the other donors' aggregate $b\ge0$, the unique best response of donor $i$ is

$$
 \operatorname{BR}_i(b)_x=(\lambda v_{ix}-b_x)_+\quad(x\in A_i),
 \qquad \sum_{x\in A_i}(\lambda v_{ix}-b_x)_+=C_i. \tag{1}
$$

All other coordinates are zero. The left side of the scalar equation is continuous, becomes strictly increasing before reaching the positive value $C_i$, and tends to infinity. For a proposed utility $\lambda$, the displayed positive parts are exactly the least expenditures attaining it. At the largest feasible utility they exhaust the budget, proving both optimality and uniqueness.

Use total-variation distance $D(p,q)=\tfrac12\|p-q\|_1$ on vectors with the same total. If $b,b'$ have the same total, then

$$
 D(\operatorname{BR}_i(b),\operatorname{BR}_i(b'))\le D(b,b'). \tag{2}
$$

To prove this, let the two levels in (1) be $\lambda\le\lambda'$, and write the two responses as $z,z'$. Whenever $z_x>z'_x$, the positive-part formula gives

$$
 0<z_x-z'_x\le(b'_x-b_x)_+.
$$

Since both responses have total $C_i$, summing their positive differences proves (2). If $\lambda\ge\lambda'$, interchange the primed and unprimed inputs. This argument permits zero external allocations and arbitrary support sets.

Set

$$
 d_i(X)=D\left(X_i,\operatorname{BR}_i\left(\sum_{j\ne i}X_j\right)\right),
 \qquad h(X)=\max_i d_i(X).
$$

These functions are continuous on the product of simplices. If a step by donor $j$ moves mass $c=D(\delta,\delta')$, then

$$
 |d_i(X')-d_i(X)|\le c \quad\hbox{for every }i. \tag{3}
$$

For $i\ne j$, use (2) and the triangle inequality; her own row is unchanged. For $i=j$, the old residual is exactly $c$ and the new residual is zero. In particular, a donor's residual at the start of any interval is at most the total mass moved before and including her next update.

## 3. A schedule condition weaker than bounded waiting

Let $t_0$ be the end of the initialization prefix. Choose consecutive finite intervals $[t_k,t_{k+1})$, each containing an update of every donor. Put $L_k=t_{k+1}-t_k$. Such intervals exist for every fair schedule; one canonical choice is to take each shortest possible complete interval.

**Theorem A.** If

$$
 \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac1{L_k}=\infty, \tag{4}
$$

then the aggregate allocations converge to the source's unique Nash-welfare allocation $\delta^*$, for arbitrary positive weights on the supports. There is no assumption that the $L_k$ are bounded.

### Proof

Consider the continuous concave function

$$
 \Phi(X)=\sum_i\sum_{x\in A_i}X_{ix}\log v_{ix}
       -\sum_x\delta_x\log\delta_x,\qquad 0\log0=0. \tag{5}
$$

For fixed other rows, (1) is the unique maximizer of (5) over row $i$. Indeed, at its solution, every positive row coordinate has the same marginal derivative

$$
 \log v_{ix}-\log(b_x+X_{ix})-1=-\log\lambda-1,
$$

and every zero row coordinate has a derivative no larger. All aggregate coordinates in $A_i$ are positive at this solution, because $\lambda>0$. These are precisely the first-order sufficient conditions for a concave maximization on the row simplex.

Every global maximizer of $\Phi$ is therefore an equilibrium. Conversely, at an equilibrium every valued aggregate coordinate is positive: a donor can secure positive utility by allocating her own budget proportionally to her weights. The rowwise first-order conditions then combine to the first-order condition over the product of simplices. Concavity makes that equilibrium a global maximizer. Thus

$$
 \{X:\Phi(X)=\Phi^*\}=\{X:h(X)=0\},\qquad
 \Phi^*=\max_X\Phi(X). \tag{6}
$$

The scalar function $s\log s-s^2/(2B)$ is convex on $[0,B]$, by its second derivative on $(0,B]$ and continuity at zero. Hence (5), as a function of any one row, is $1/B$-strongly concave in the Euclidean norm. A maximizing row update gives

$$
 \Phi(X^{t+1})-\Phi(X^t)
 \ge\frac{\|X_{i_t}^{t+1}-X_{i_t}^{t}\|_2^2}{2B}
 \ge\frac{2c_t^2}{Bm},\qquad
 c_t=D(\delta^{t+1},\delta^t). \tag{7}
$$

The first inequality also follows directly by applying strong concavity to the segment from the maximizing row to the old row and taking a one-sided limit; it does not require differentiability at the old state.

By (3), the total moved mass in a complete interval is at least the largest residual at its start. Cauchy–Schwarz and (7) therefore give

$$
 \Phi(X^{t_{k+1}})-\Phi(X^{t_k})
 \ge\frac{2}{BmL_k}\left(\sum_{t=t_k}^{t_{k+1}-1}c_t\right)^2
 \ge\frac{2h(X^{t_k})^2}{BmL_k}. \tag{8}
$$

The nondecreasing potential has a finite limit $\ell\le\Phi^*$. Suppose $\ell<\Phi^*$. On the nonempty compact set

$$
 \{X:\Phi(X)\le(\ell+\Phi^*)/2\}
$$

the continuous function $h$ has a strictly positive minimum $\eta$, by (6). All sufficiently late states lie in this set. Summing (8) and using (4) would make $\Phi$ unbounded. This contradiction proves $\Phi(X^t)\to\Phi^*$.

Every accumulation point of the states maximizes $\Phi$, so it is an equilibrium by (6). All equilibrium aggregates equal $\delta^*$. This uniqueness also follows directly from (5): two maximizers with different aggregates would have a midpoint with strictly larger potential, by strict concavity of the entropy term. Compactness then implies $\delta^t\to\delta^*$. $\square$

For example, $L_k\le C(k+1)$, or even $L_k\le C(k+2)\log(k+2)$, is sufficient. With at least three donors, alternating two donors $k+1$ times before updating the remaining donors gives complete intervals of linearly growing length and unbounded waiting. Formula (4) is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one: no nonconvergence is claimed when its sum is finite.

## 4. All fair schedules for consistent project weights

**Theorem B.** Suppose that positive numbers $a_i,w_x$ exist such that

$$
 v_{ix}=a_iw_x\quad\hbox{whenever }x\in A_i. \tag{9}
$$

Then every fair best-response schedule converges. In fact, every individual donor allocation converges, and the total mass moved after initialization is finite. The limiting aggregate is $\delta^*$.

### Proof

The factor $a_i$ can be canceled from donor $i$'s preferences. Define

$$
 G_w(\delta)=\sum_{x<y}|w_y\delta_x-w_x\delta_y|
 =\sum_{x<y}w_xw_y\left|\frac{\delta_x}{w_x}-\frac{\delta_y}{w_y}\right|,
 \qquad w_{\min}=\min_x w_x>0. \tag{10}
$$

Consider a transfer of mass $\varepsilon>0$ from $x$ to $y$ that does not reverse the order of their normalized allocations:

$$
 \frac{\delta_x-\varepsilon}{w_x}\ge
 \frac{\delta_y+\varepsilon}{w_y}. \tag{11}
$$

The $x,y$ term of (10) decreases by exactly $(w_x+w_y)\varepsilon$. For every third project $z$, the sum of the $x,z$ and $y,z$ terms cannot increase. Away from its finitely many breakpoints its derivative during the transfer is

$$
 w_z\left[\operatorname{sgn}\left(\frac{\delta_y+s}{w_y}-\frac{\delta_z}{w_z}\right)
 -\operatorname{sgn}\left(\frac{\delta_x-s}{w_x}-\frac{\delta_z}{w_z}\right)\right]\le0,
 \quad 0\le s\le\varepsilon.
$$

Continuity handles the breakpoints. Consequently

$$
 G_w(\delta)-G_w(\delta-\varepsilon e_x+\varepsilon e_y)
 \ge (w_x+w_y)\varepsilon\ge2w_{\min}\varepsilon. \tag{12}
$$

Every best response is a finite sum of transfers satisfying (11), whose transferred amounts sum to the moved mass $c_t$. To see this without assuming a particular water-filling implementation, pair coordinates where the donor's row decreases with coordinates where it increases, transferring until one required change is exhausted. At the final best response, all recipient coordinates have normalized aggregate equal to its water level, while every source coordinate has normalized aggregate at least that level. At an intermediate stage a source is at least its final value and a recipient is at most its final value. Thus (11) holds throughout. Each pairing exhausts a required coordinate change, so finitely many suffice.

Summing (12) over these transfers and then over the updates gives

$$
 \sum_{t=t_0}^{\infty}c_t\le\frac{G_w(\delta^{t_0})}{2w_{\min}}. \tag{13}
$$

Only one row changes per step, with $\ell^1$ change $2c_t$. Therefore the full vector $X^t$ has finite total variation and converges to a state $X^\infty$. For each donor, fairness supplies a subsequence of times immediately after her updates. Her residual is zero at every such time; continuity of $d_i$ gives $d_i(X^\infty)=0$. The limit is an equilibrium, and its aggregate is $\delta^*$. $\square$

### A combinatorial criterion for the weights

Build the bipartite incidence graph with donor vertices $i$, project vertices $x$, and edge $ix$ whenever $x\in A_i$. Condition (9) holds exactly when every cycle

$$
 i_1,x_1,i_2,x_2,\ldots,i_r,x_r,i_1
$$

satisfies

$$
 \prod_{j=1}^r v_{i_jx_j}=\prod_{j=1}^r v_{i_{j+1}x_j},\qquad i_{r+1}=i_1. \tag{14}
$$

Necessity follows by cancellation. For sufficiency, choose one positive factor at a root of each connected component, propagate $v_{ix}=a_iw_x$ along a spanning tree, and use (14) to verify every remaining edge. In particular, an incidence forest has no cycle conditions: **all positive weight assignments on an incidence forest satisfy Theorem B**.

This includes genuinely unequal within-donor weights and heterogeneous support sets. The binary case is recovered by $w_x=1$, but was already proved in the source.

## 5. What remains unresolved

Theorem A does not cover every fair schedule; exponentially growing complete intervals can have a convergent reciprocal sum. Theorem B does not cover profiles with inconsistent weight products around an incidence cycle. Neither limitation implies that the original conjecture is false.

For arbitrary profiles, (7) proves square-summability of the moved masses. It does not prove summability, and Cauchy–Schwarz over unbounded intervals alone does not remove condition (4). The linear estimate (12) relies on the common project factors in (9). These are the precise missing steps toward a full answer.
